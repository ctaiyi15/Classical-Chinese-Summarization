Lithos
Wang Xiang, whose courtesy name was Xiuzheng, was born in Linyi, Langye, and was a descendant of Wang Ji, an admonishing official of the Han Dynasty.
His grandfather, Wang Ren, was the governor of Qingzhou.
His father, Wang Rong, was recruited by the government, but he refused to become an official.
Wang Xiang is very filial by nature.
His mother died when he was young, and his stepmother Zhu was not loving and spoke ill of him many times, thus losing his father's love.
Wang Xiang is often asked to clean the cattle pen, and Wang Xiang is more respectful and cautious.
When his parents were ill, he couldn't take off his clothes, so he had to taste the medicine himself first.
His stepmother once wanted to eat live fish. It was freezing cold at that time. Wang Xiang took off his clothes and was about to break the ice to catch fish. The ice suddenly cracked on its own and two carps jumped out. He took the fish home.
Stepmother wants to eat roasted oriole again. Dozens more orioles flew into his tent and took them to his stepmother.
The villagers were amazed and thought it was caused by the influence of filial piety.
There was a red sand fruit tree bearing fruit, and his stepmother ordered him to guard it. Whenever there was wind or rain, Wang Xiang would hug the tree and cry.
His filial piety reaches such an extent.
During the war at the end of the Han Dynasty, he supported his mother and took his younger brother Wang Lan to escape to Lujiang. They lived in seclusion for more than 20 years and refused to accept appointments from prefectures and counties.
When her mother passed away, she became ill due to grief and could only stand with a cane.

summary:
summary line 0: Wang Xiang, courtesy name Xiuzheng, was from Linyi, Langye, a descendant of Han official Wang Ji. His grandfather Wang Ren served as Qingzhou governor, while his father Wang Rong refused government service. Wang Xiang was exceptionally filial. After his mother died young, his stepmother Zhu treated him poorly and spoke ill of him, causing his father to lose affection for him. Despite being ordered to clean cattle pens, Wang Xiang remained respectful. When his parents fell ill, he personally tasted their medicine. During freezing weather, his stepmother desired live fish; Wang Xiang removed his clothes to break ice, and two carps leaped out spontaneously. When she wanted roasted oriole, dozens flew into his tent. Villagers attributed these events to his filial piety. During late Han warfare, he supported his mother and younger brother Wang Lan while hiding in Lujiang for over twenty years, refusing official appointments. After his mother's death, he became ill from grief. At nearly sixty, he initially refused Governor Lu Qian's invitation but eventually accepted after Wang Lan's persuasion. He defeated bandits, brought peace to the state, and was praised in song. Wang Xiang held various official positions including Wen Ling, Da Sinong, and was named Guannei Marquis. He served as Sili Xiaowei, Taichang, and Marquis of Wansui Ting. The emperor appointed him as third elder at the Imperial Academy, where he taught political education. He was promoted to Sikong, Taiwei, and Shizhong, eventually becoming Suiling Marquis with 1,600 households. When Emperor Wen became King of Jin, Wang Xiang refused to kneel, stating that as a Wei official, he would not bow to others. Emperor Wu appointed him Taibao and promoted him to Duke. Despite repeated retirement requests due to age, the emperor refused, granting him privileges including exemption from court attendance. Upon critical illness, Wang Xiang instructed his descendants on simple burial, forbidding elaborate ceremonies and expensive items. He died in the fourth year of Taishi at age eighty-five. His sons included Wang Zhao, Wang Xia, Wang Fu, Wang Lie, and Wang Fen.

When Emperor Wen of Wei was the crown prince, he searched for and promoted lowly talents, appointed Zheng Chong as the literary minister, and was promoted to Shangshulang many times. He also went to Beijing to replace Chenliu as the prefect.
Zheng Chong regards elegance as his virtue. He does not have the reputation of being talented or magnanimous in his position. He only needs food and clothing and does not seek wealth. The world values ​​him for this.
General Cao Shuang recommended him to serve as a lieutenant, and then he was promoted to a regular attendant of Sanqi and Guang Luxun.
In the third year of Jiaping's reign, he was appointed Sikong.
When the Duke of Gaogui was giving lectures on Shangshu, Zheng Chong took the scriptures and taught them in person, and was rewarded together with his attendant Zheng Xiaotong.
Soon he was transferred to Situ.
Chang Daoxiang came to the throne and appointed him as Taibao, ranking above the three divisions, and named him Shouguang Hou.
Although Zheng Chong's official rank is the same as that of prime minister, he does not interfere in world affairs.
At that time, Emperor Wen assisted the government and after pacifying the Shu Kingdom, he ordered Jia Chong, Yang Hu and others to formulate etiquette and legal regulations respectively. They all asked Zheng Chong first and then implemented them.
When Emperor Wei announced his abdication, he sent Zheng Chong to present the policy letter.
Emperor Wu ascended the throne, appointed him as Taifu, and promoted him to Duke.
Soon, Si Li Yi and Zhong Cheng Hou Shi Guang wrote a letter saying that Zheng Chong, He Zeng, Xun Hao and others should be relieved of their official positions due to illness.
The emperor did not allow it.
Zheng Chong then stopped dealing with political affairs and asked to resign.
He issued an edict to dissuade him with good words, and sent someone to explain.
Zheng Chong resolutely refused and handed over Diao Chan's seal ribbon, but the edict did not allow it.

summary:
summary line 1: Zheng Chong, courtesy name Wenhe, was from Kaifeng, Rongyang. Born poor, he established moral integrity, was quiet with few desires, and extensively studied Confucian classics. He followed etiquette strictly and did not seek local fame. Emperor Wen of Wei appointed him literary minister, later promoting him to Shangshulang and Chenliu prefect. He valued elegance over wealth, earning respect. Cao Shuang recommended him as lieutenant, then Sanqi Changshi and Guang Luxun. In Jiaping's third year, he became Sikong, later Situ. He was appointed Taibao and named Shouguang Hou. Though holding prime ministerial rank, he avoided worldly affairs. When Emperor Wei abdicated, Zheng Chong presented the policy letter. Emperor Wu appointed him Taifu and promoted him to Duke. Despite illness-related requests for removal, the emperor refused. In the sixth year of Taishi, the emperor praised Zheng Chong, He Zeng, and Xun Hao for their virtue and service. Zheng Chong repeatedly resigned, and the emperor eventually allowed him to return to his mansion as Duke Shouguang with full privileges. He died the following year, receiving posthumous honors. Zheng Chong compiled "Collected Commentary of the Analects of Confucius" with others. Having no sons, his nephew Zheng Hui inherited his title.
