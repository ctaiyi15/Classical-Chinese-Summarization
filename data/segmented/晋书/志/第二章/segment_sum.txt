summary:
summary line 2: The text also covers comets, guest stars, meteors, and various atmospheric phenomena (clouds, rainbows, halos) as omens. Comets, or "broom stars," signify sweeping away the old and ushering in the new, often bringing war and floods. Specific comets like the "Red You Flag" or "Heavenly Spear" portend rebellion, military defeat, or the death of a ruler. Guest stars like the "Old Man Star" or "Zhou Bo Star" indicate famine or prosperity. Meteors, as "angels," foretell events based on their size, speed, and direction; their fall marks war or the death of a general. Atmospheric qi, such as the "Emperor's Qi" (red inside, yellow outside) or the "Qi of a Fierce General" (like a dragon or beast), signal the rise of a king or a military leader. Cloud formations, like "formation clouds" or "hook clouds," predict war, and their colors (white for mourning, red for war, etc.) determine the nature of the event.

summary:
summary line 3: The text concludes with historical records of celestial events and their supposed fulfillments, such as solar eclipses, sunspots, and planetary conjunctions, linked to specific dynastic events like usurpations, deaths of emperors, and military campaigns. For instance, a solar eclipse in the second year of Emperor Hui's Yuankang reign was interpreted as a sign of the empress's exclusive power. The gathering of five stars in a constellation was considered a sign of a change in dynasty, as when the stars gathered in Dongjing before Liu Yu's conquest of Guanzhong and the subsequent transition from Jin to Song. The text emphasizes that these celestial signs are warnings from Heaven, urging rulers to cultivate virtue and correct their governance to avert disaster.
