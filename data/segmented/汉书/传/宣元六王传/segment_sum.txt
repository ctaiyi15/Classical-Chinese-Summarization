summary:
summary line 0: Emperor Xiaoxuan had five sons. The empress gave birth to Emperor Xiaoyuan, while other consorts bore Huaiyang Xianwang Liu Qin, Chu Xiaowang Liu Xiao, Dongping Siwang Liu Yu, and Zhongshan Aiwang Liu Tong, who died young. Liu Qin, made king in the third year of Yuankang, was favored by Emperor Xuan due to his mother Zhang Shidan’s influence. After Empress Huo was deposed, Xuan initially wanted to make Zhang queen but feared for the crown prince’s safety, instead choosing Wang Jiantun, a childless and cautious woman, to raise the prince. Zhang remained the most favored consort. Liu Qin, intelligent and fond of scriptures, was admired by Xuan, who considered replacing the crown prince but relented due to the prince’s early loss of his mother. After Xuan’s death, Emperor Yuan ordered Liu Qin to his fief. Zhang Shidan died, and Liu Qin’s maternal uncle Zhang Bo and his brothers were rewarded for visits. Liu Qin requested the Zhang family’s relocation to Huaiyang, but Zhang Bo refused, causing resentment. Zhang Bo later demanded debt repayment and, after receiving gold, flattered Liu Qin in letters, urging him to seek court audience and assist the emperor. Liu Qin initially declined but eventually agreed, sending gold and gifts. Zhang Bo’s son-in-law Jingfang, favored by Emperor Yuan for his knowledge of the *I Ching*, shared court secrets with Zhang Bo, who fabricated claims of influence with officials. Liu Qin was persuaded to pursue audience, but Jingfang’s departure and Shi Xian’s exposure led to arrests. The emperor, sparing Liu Qin, sent an admonishing official to rebuke him, citing his correspondence with Zhang Bo as treasonous. Liu Qin apologized, and Zhang Bo and his brothers were exiled. Under Emperor Cheng, Liu Qin requested the exiles’ return, which was granted despite official objections. He died in his thirty-sixth year, succeeded by his son Liu Xuan, then grandson Liu Yan, whose line ended under Wang Mang.
