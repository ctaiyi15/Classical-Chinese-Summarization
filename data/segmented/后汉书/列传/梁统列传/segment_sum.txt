He submitted a memorial arguing that lenient punishments under Emperors Yuan and Ai had led to lawlessness and increased crime. He cited historical precedents from the Five Emperors, Three Kings, and Han emperors, urging the adoption of stricter measures. However, the matter was referred to the Sangong and Tingwei, who deemed harsh punishments unnecessary and refused to change the old law. Liang Tong wrote again, clarifying he did not seek severe punishment but adherence to old rules. He argued that mitigating punishment had caused rampant theft and rebellion, citing recent outbreaks of banditry. His memorial was sent but not reported. Later, Liang Tong served as Prefect of Jiujiang and was granted the title of Marquis of Lingxiang. Liang Ji's power grew unchecked, with all court matters decided by him. He killed officials who opposed him, including Wu Shu and Hou Meng. A young doctor named Yuan Zhu wrote to the emperor criticizing Liang Ji's power, but Liang Ji had him beaten to death. Liang Ji also killed many others, including Hao Xie and Hu Wu's families. Liang Buyi, who loved scriptures and treated others well, was estranged by Liang Ji and resigned. Liang Ji's family included seven marquises, three queens, six nobles, two generals, and many other officials. Liang Ji held power for over 20 years, dominating the court. Emperor Huan, angered by Liang Ji's arrogance, plotted with eunuchs to kill him. In the first year of Yanxi, after a series of events, Emperor Huan ordered the arrest of Liang Ji. Liang Ji and Sun Shou committed suicide. Over 300 of his officials and guests were dismissed. His property, worth over three billion, was confiscated and used to reduce taxes. His gardens were given to the poor.
